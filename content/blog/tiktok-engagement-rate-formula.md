There is no useful TikTok engagement-rate percentage without a named denominator.

A rate calculated with followers answers a different question from one calculated with video views. Both can be useful estimates, but they should never be presented as interchangeable or as a single official TikTok metric.

If you want to calculate a result now, use the [TikTok Engagement Rate Calculator](/tiktok-engagement-rate-calculator). It uses the follower-based formula and shows the calculation beside the result.

## The short answer

Use **engagement rate by followers** when your question is:

> How large are the average interactions on my sampled posts relative to the account's current follower count?

Use **engagement rate by views** when your question is:

> How many visible interactions occurred relative to the views generated by the sampled posts?

The two formulas are:

`Follower-based rate = (Average likes + Average comments + Average shares) ÷ Followers × 100`

`View-based rate = (Total likes + Total comments + Total shares) ÷ Total views × 100`

Before comparing two results, make sure they use the same denominator, interaction types, post sample and observation dates.

If you already have two different percentages, use [Why TikTok Engagement Calculators Show Different Results](/blog/why-tiktok-engagement-calculators-differ) to audit the calculations step by step.

## Formula 1: engagement rate by followers

TokLens' standalone calculator uses:

`(Average likes + Average comments + Average shares) ÷ Followers × 100`

This formula compares the average visible interactions on a post sample with the account's current follower count.

### What you need

- Current follower count.
- Average likes per post.
- Average comments per post.
- Average shares per post.
- The number of posts and dates used to produce those averages.

All three interaction averages must come from the same set of posts. Do not use average likes from the latest 20 posts and average comments from the latest five.

For a complete input worksheet covering totals, averages, genuine zeros and unavailable fields, read [What Counts as TikTok Engagement in a Calculator?](/blog/what-counts-as-tiktok-engagement-in-calculator).

### Worked follower-based example

Suppose an illustrative account has:

- 25,000 followers.
- 1,200 average likes.
- 80 average comments.
- 40 average shares.

First, add the average interactions:

`1,200 + 80 + 40 = 1,320`

Then divide by followers and multiply by 100:

`1,320 ÷ 25,000 × 100 = 5.28%`

The result is an **estimated follower-based engagement rate of 5.28%** for the selected sample. It is not an official TikTok rating, a promise of future performance or proof that 5.28% of followers interacted.

The denominator is the current follower count, while the numerator is an average built from posts published over a period. Record both the sample dates and the date when the follower count was collected.

## Formula 2: engagement rate by views

A view-based calculation uses:

`(Total likes + Total comments + Total shares) ÷ Total views × 100`

This compares visible interactions with the observed reach of the same post sample. It is often more useful when the question concerns how viewers responded after the posts were viewed.

### What you need

- Total likes across the sampled posts.
- Total comments across the same posts.
- Total shares across the same posts.
- Total views across the same posts.
- The number of posts and observation dates.

TikTok's current developer documentation defines `like_count`, `comment_count`, `share_count` and `view_count` as video fields. Access and availability still depend on the relevant product, authorization and content. The presence of those fields does not make a calculation based on them an official TikTok engagement-rate standard.

### Worked view-based example

Use the same illustrative ten-post sample:

- 12,000 total likes.
- 800 total comments.
- 400 total shares.
- 180,000 total views.

Add the interactions:

`12,000 + 800 + 400 = 13,200`

Then divide by views:

`13,200 ÷ 180,000 × 100 = 7.33%`

The sample therefore has an **estimated view-based engagement rate of 7.33%**.

The 7.33% result does not contradict the 5.28% follower-based result. The numerators describe the same illustrative posts, but the denominators answer different questions.

## Which formula should you use?

Choose the formula from the decision you need to make.

### Use followers when:

- You only have an account-level follower count and average post interactions.
- You want a consistent high-level estimate across accounts.
- Every account is calculated with the same interaction types and sample rules.
- You keep the denominator clearly labelled.

### Use views when:

- You have view and interaction totals for the same posts.
- You want to describe visible interactions relative to observed views.
- Accounts have very different reach relative to their follower counts.
- You can disclose the exact post sample.

### Use neither as a standalone decision when:

- The sample contains too few posts to represent normal performance.
- One unusual post dominates the result.
- You do not know whether missing counts were treated as zero.
- The accounts were measured over different windows.
- Your decision depends on audience fit, watch time, retention, conversions or brand safety.

For campaign research, calculate the same formula for every creator and then add qualitative and first-party context. [Engagement rate alone cannot establish reach, consistency, audience fit or business outcomes](/blog/when-tiktok-engagement-rate-is-not-enough).

## Weighted rate versus averaging post percentages

Two view-based methods can produce different answers.

The recommended account-sample calculation is:

`Total interactions across all sampled posts ÷ Total views across all sampled posts × 100`

This weights posts by their number of views.

Another method calculates a percentage for every post and then averages those percentages:

`Sum of individual post engagement rates ÷ Number of posts`

That method gives a low-view post the same weight as a high-view post. It is not automatically wrong, but it answers a different question. Reports should state which method they use.

Do not compare a weighted account rate with an average of post-level rates as if they were calculated identically.

## How to prepare a fair post sample

The formula is simple. Selecting the sample is where many misleading comparisons begin.

For a worked comparison showing how the same account changes across 3, 5 and 10-post windows, read [How Sample Size Changes a TikTok Engagement-Rate Estimate](/blog/how-sample-size-changes-tiktok-engagement-rate).

### 1. Use one post set

Likes, comments, shares and views must all come from the same posts.

### 2. Record the number of posts

“Latest posts” is incomplete. Write “latest 10 public posts collected on July 26, 2026,” or another exact description.

### 3. Record the dates

Counts change. The collection date is part of the result.

### 4. Treat missing and zero differently

Zero is a known count. Unavailable means the value was not obtained. Turning unavailable shares into zero understates the numerator.

### 5. Check post age

A post published two hours ago has had less time to accumulate views and interactions than a post published two weeks ago.

### 6. Keep inclusion rules consistent

Decide how to handle pinned posts, unavailable posts, promoted content and formats that do not expose equivalent fields. Apply the same rule to every account being compared.

## Common calculation mistakes

### Mixing totals with averages

Do not add total likes across ten posts to average comments per post. Convert every interaction to totals or every interaction to averages before calculating.

### Hiding the denominator

Writing only “engagement rate: 5.28%” prevents the reader from reproducing or interpreting the result. Label it `by followers` or `by views`.

### Comparing different windows

An account measured across three posts should not be treated as directly equivalent to one measured across thirty posts without explaining the difference.

### Treating unavailable data as zero

Missing data is a limitation, not a zero-value interaction.

### Calling the result official

TokLens calculates an estimate from the inputs or authorized fields available to it. The result is not supplied, verified or endorsed by TikTok.

### Treating engagement as business performance

Likes, comments and shares do not reveal purchases, revenue, audience demographics or whether a comment is positive. The rate is one descriptive signal.

## What these formulas do not measure

Neither formula proves:

- Watch time or completion rate.
- Audience demographics or geographic fit.
- Saves when that field is not included.
- Link clicks, leads, purchases or revenue.
- Positive sentiment.
- Organic versus promoted distribution.
- Why TikTok distributed a post.
- Future performance.

Creators should use first-party TikTok analytics for private account insights that are not represented by these visible counts. A third-party estimate should supplement that context, not imitate it.

## A comparison checklist

Before placing two engagement-rate percentages side by side, confirm:

- The denominator is the same.
- The included interaction types are the same.
- The post count is disclosed.
- The date window is comparable.
- Every metric comes from the same post sample.
- Missing values are labelled unavailable.
- The calculation method is the same.
- Both results are labelled estimated.
- Relevant qualitative and first-party context is considered separately.

If any item differs, explain it before drawing a conclusion.
